## The Bear Read on a Low Multiple

The market's argument for the depressed PE is not irrational. Here it is in its strongest form:

### The Depreciation Hit Is Coming

The earnings power being built right now shows up in the income statement with a lag. The $128B Amazon is spending this year depreciates over 4-6 years. So:

- FY2025 capex starts depreciating in earnest in FY2026-FY2027
- FY2026 capex ($200B guided) depreciates from FY2027 onward
- D&A run rates for Amazon were $55B TTM as of Q1 2025 and climbing

The bear case: TTM earnings look okay *now* because the depreciation hasn't fully hit. But FY2026-FY2028 earnings will be compressed by the depreciation schedule from current capex levels. If you're buying at 32x on TTM earnings, you're paying for earnings that are about to get quieter, not louder.

The same math applies to MSFT: D&A nearly doubled in three years ($14.5B → $34.2B). If revenue doesn't keep pace with the depreciation schedule, earnings estimates will need to come down. The forward PE of 23x looks cheap until you realize the denominator might shrink before it grows.

### FCF vs GAAP Earnings Divergence

Amazon's FCF collapsed from $38B to $11B. The market may be correctly anticipating that GAAP earnings will eventually follow FCF lower, not because the business is failing, but because the accounting hasn't caught up to the cash reality yet.

### The RPO-to-Revenue Conversion Risk

$244B in Amazon's backlog and $368B in Microsoft's commercial RPO are real. But the recognition timeline is spread over 4+ years. If contracts are renegotiated, if customer demand shifts, or if competition erodes pricing, the backlog doesn't convert on schedule. A 4.1-year weighted average life is only an assumption.

## The Bull Case for Current Valuation

### The Multiple Is Cheap If Earnings Grow Into It

At 27x FY2026 earnings, MSFT is pricing in ~16% annual EPS growth. That's what analysts are estimating. If it hits, the stock is fairly valued, not cheap. But if AI monetization accelerates (as Jassy argues it will), estimates could move higher. At 27x with 20%+ growth, it's inexpensive. At 27x with 16% growth, it's fair.

For AMZN, the path is similar: if AWS continues at 20%+ growth and the capex normalizes as supply catches demand, FY2027 earnings of $10.25+ puts the stock at ~26x forward — cheap for a company that was $8.36 TTM.

### Capex Is Not a Cost, It's an Investment

The companies are financing assets that generate returns for 15+ years. Microsoft describes their data center investment as having "15 years and beyond" of monetization horizon. Amazon's AWS in 2014 faced the same "earnings will be compressed" argument and emerged with $129B in annual revenue. If the AI buildout is analogous, current earnings are not representative of normalized earnings power.

### Operating Cash Flow Is the Floor

Despite the FCF collapse, Amazon generated $139.5B in operating cash flow in FY2025. Microsoft generated $136.2B. These are not businesses in distress — they're businesses choosing to invest cash flow back into the asset base rather than return it to shareholders. The cash is being deployed, not destroyed.

## The Moat: How Much Does It Take to Compete?

### The Minimum Capex Floor to Be a Player

Based on available analysis:

| Tier | Annual Capex Required | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyperscaler** | $20B+ | Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta |
| **Tier 2 viable** | $5-10B | Oracle, CoreWeave (~$7-10B), some telcos |
| **Infrastructure debt** | <$1B | Below this, you cannot stay competitive |

A single hyperscale AI data center (500-1000 MW) costs **$4-10B** to construct. GPUs alone run $30,000-$40,000 per chip. Custom silicon development costs **$1-10B per generation**. Networking infrastructure (InfiniBand at ~$200K per GPU interconnect) adds another layer of cost.

The floor to remain relevant in AI infrastructure is $5-10B annually. The cost to lead is $50B+.

### The Reinforcing Loop

The structural advantages compound because:

1. **Scale → Unit cost reduction.** AWS, Azure, and GCP negotiate GPU purchases at 20-40% discounts vs. smaller buyers. Every $10B in capex creates a procurement advantage that smaller players can't replicate.

2. **Capacity → Utilization → Pricing power.** Hyperscalers maintain 60-80% GPU utilization vs. 30-50% for smaller players, meaning lower per-unit cost and better margins to reinvest.

3. **Customers → Switching costs.** Enterprises building AI products on specific clouds (fine-tuning models, RAG implementations, data gravity) face massive migration costs. Every quarter of use deepens the lock-in.

4. **Revenue → More capex.** AWS generates $129B annually; AWS reinvests much of it. Microsoft Azure generates $37B+ run rate; it reinvests. The cash generation itself becomes a competitive moat because it funds the next cycle of infrastructure.

5. **Power contracts → Multi-decade advantages.** Hyperscalers have secured 10-15 year power purchase agreements at favorable rates. A new entrant in 2026 is competing not just on hardware but on power availability that was allocated years ago.

### The Custom Silicon Layer

| Company | Chip | Moat |
|---|---|---|
| Google | TPU v5e/v6 | 2-4x training efficiency over merchant silicon for their workloads |
| Amazon | Trainium 2 | 30-40% cost-performance vs. H100 |
| Microsoft | Maia 100 / Cobalt | Azure-optimized, office workloads |
| Meta | MTIA | Inference optimization |

Custom silicon reduces per-flop cost by 30-50% vs. merchant silicon. This isn't just a cost advantage — it's a product advantage, because lower inference costs can be passed to customers or used to undercut competition on price.

### Can Smaller Players Compete?

**The case for niche play:**
- CoreWeave (~$7-10B capex) is IPOing as a specialized GPU cloud. Their focus on GPU infrastructure achieves 85%+ utilization in their niche.
- Oracle (~$10-15B) has AI deals with Microsoft and competes in specific verticals.
- Open source (Meta's Llama models) reduces the importance of frontier model capabilities — training can happen on smaller infrastructure.

**The case for continued concentration:**
- Training GPT-4 class models requires 25,000+ H100s for ~90 days. Only Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon have this capacity.
- InfiniBand networking requires $1B+ investments per cluster. Small players can't amortize it.
- ML infrastructure talent is scarce — hyperscalers have 10,000+ engineers vs. hundreds for tier-2 players.

**The bottom line:** The moat is real and self-reinforcing. Custom chips, power contracts, customer relationships, and talent create compounding advantages that are not easily replicated. Niche competitors can survive (CoreWeave model) but the full-stack AI infrastructure market remains concentrated among four companies spending $40B+ annually.

## Key Takeaways

1. **AMZN at ~32x TTM, ~27-30x FY2026; MSFT at ~27x TTM, ~23x FY2026.** Neither is cheap in a traditional sense, but both are trading below their 2024 peaks as capex concerns weigh on sentiment.

2. **The bear case on PE is legitimate:** Earnings haven't fully reflected the depreciation hit yet. TTM earnings look better than normalized earnings because the accounting lags the cash reality by 4-6 years. If you buy at 32x on TTM, you're potentially paying for earnings that compress before they grow.

3. **The bull case is also legitimate:** OCF is strong, backlog is growing, and the AI revenue ramp (if it plays out) means current earnings are not representative of 3-5 year earnings power. Jassy's historical parallel is real — AWS was dismissed as capex-destroying in 2014 and emerged as a $129B business.

4. **The valuation question is ultimately a bet on the AI revenue ramp.** At 27-32x, the market is not pricing in failure — it's pricing in uncertainty. If AI monetization accelerates, estimates go up and the multiple compresses to a buy. If it stalls, estimates go down and the PE looks expensive on a forward basis.

5. **The moat is real and self-reinforcing.** $20B+ annual capex is the floor to compete; $50B+ to lead. The scale loop (more spend → more capacity → more customers → more revenue → more spend) benefits the incumbents and makes it harder for new entrants to catch up.
